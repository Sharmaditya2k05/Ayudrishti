Imagine starting yoga at home. An app shows you a video of someone doing a perfect pose — and then you're on your own. Is your back straight? Is your knee in a safe position? Are you actually getting better week by week? A video can't tell you, because it never looks at you.

AyuDrishti closes that gap. It uses your phone's camera to watch how you do a pose, tells you out loud exactly what to fix, keeps score, and remembers — so tomorrow's session is shaped by how today actually went. Below, we build up the whole thing one simple step at a time, and explain every new word the moment it appears.

Under one simple idea — watch, correct, remember, adapt — the project quietly pulls together a lot: computer vision that runs on your own phone, a hand-built scoring system with 82 checks across 10 poses, spoken hands-free coaching in English and Hindi, a plan that rewrites itself from your history, a yoga chatbot that only answers from trusted notes, and an optional sensor band. The final part opens the hood on exactly which technologies do this and how each one was wired in and tuned.

Part 1

The big idea: a loop, not a video

Most yoga apps are a one-way street: they show, you copy. AyuDrishti is a loop — a circle where each step feeds the next, and the whole thing keeps improving. It works like a real teacher's routine:

The one-line version

A photo app can say “this is Warrior II.” AyuDrishti says “Warrior II — 84%. Your front knee has drifted past your ankle; draw it back.” That extra sentence is the whole point.

Part 2

How it watches you — without seeing you

Here's the part that surprises people: your photo or video never leaves your phone. Instead, the phone quietly finds 33 key points on your body — head, shoulders, elbows, hips, knees, ankles — and only the positions of those dots (just numbers) are sent onward. The picture stays with you.

In plain words
Landmark

One of the 33 dots the phone places on your body to describe your shape.

Like a connect-the-dots outline of you — the app keeps the dots and throws the photo away.
In plain words
MediaPipe

The free Google technology that finds those 33 dots in the camera image, right on your device.

Part 3

Naming a pose vs judging it

This is the heart of the project. There are two very different questions you can ask about a pose, and most apps only answer the first one:

Recognition — “which pose?”

Names the shape. “This is Warrior II.” Useful, but it tells a struggling beginner nothing about how they're doing.

Assessment — “how well?”

Grades it and coaches. “Warrior II — 84%. Front knee too far forward; draw it back.” This is what a teacher does.

AyuDrishti does both, but it keeps them separate on purpose. If it used its strict grading rules to recognise the pose, a wobbly beginner's Warrior II might not be recognised as Warrior II at all — and they'd get no help. So recognition uses loosened rules (match the rough shape), while assessment uses the strict ones (grade the quality).

In plain words
Recognition vs Assessment

Recognition = what pose is this. Assessment = how good is it and what to fix.

A friend can name the song. A music teacher tells you which note you missed.
Part 4

How it turns a pose into a score

The app doesn't have one big mysterious opinion. Instead, each pose has its own little checklist, and every item on it is one thing a good teacher would watch. Add up the checklist and you get the score — and because it's a checklist, the app can always explain why.

In plain words
Alignment rule

One checklist item for one body part — for example, “the front knee should sit directly above the ankle.” Warrior II has 11 such rules; Mountain Pose has 10.

Each rule has a good zone (the target range). Sit inside it and you get full marks for that item. Drift outside and you lose marks gradually — a knee slightly off looks nothing like a knee badly off. The distance you're allowed to drift before scoring zero is the tolerance.

It refuses to guess

If the camera can't clearly see a body part, the app drops the rules that need it and lowers its stated confidence — instead of inventing a number. A score you can trust beats a score that's always there.

Part 5

Telling you what to fix — out loud

Your hands are on the mat, not the screen — so AyuDrishti is built to be used hands-free. It listens for a few simple words and it talks back:

  • Say “ready” — it starts checking the pose you're holding.
  • It speaks the fix aloud — “lower your shoulders, lengthen your spine.”
  • Say “retry” to try again, or “next” to move on.

It also speaks two languages — English and Hindi — and everything works with normal buttons too, for when voice isn't available.

In plain words
Text-to-speech (TTS) & voice commands

Text-to-speech = the app reading a correction out loud. Voice commands = the app listening for words like “ready” and “next.” Both use tools already built into the web browser — nothing extra to install.

Part 6

Choosing your practice — and changing it over time

Before you even start, the app builds today's session from your goals, experience level, and past scores — and it shows its reasoning for every pose. Nothing is a black box.

In plain words
Adaptation

After each session the app looks at what you struggled with, and feeds that back into the next plan. A pose you keep finding hard comes back around on purpose.

This is the loop from Part 1 closing — today's practice is literally shaped by yesterday's measurements.
Part 7

The yoga chatbot that doesn't make things up

There's a chat assistant you can ask anything about yoga — poses, breathing, meditation, safety. But it's built to be honest. Instead of answering from a vague memory, it first looks up the relevant pages from AyuDrishti's own yoga notebook, then answers using only those pages — and shows you which pages it used. This approach has a name: RAG.

In plain words
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

A way of using AI where it retrieves (looks up) relevant facts first, then generates (writes) an answer grounded in them.

Open-book, not from-memory — so it's far less likely to make things up.
In plain words
LLM & Groq

An LLM (large language model) is the AI that writes replies in normal sentences. Groq is the fast, free-tier service that runs it. AyuDrishti supplies its own notes; the LLM just does the wording.

Part 8

The optional wrist band

For people who want more, there's an optional wearable band — a tiny do-it-yourself gadget that reads your heart rate, blood-oxygen, sweat level, and movement. It's completely optional; the camera side works fully without it.

In plain words
ESP32 & Bluetooth (BLE)

ESP32 is a cheap, tiny programmable computer inside the band. Bluetooth (BLE) is the short-range wireless it uses to send readings to your phone.

Not a medical device

The band is a wellness gadget made from hobby sensors. It never diagnoses anything — the strongest thing it ever does is gently suggest you pause and rest.

Part 9

How it's all put together

Under the hood there are three main parts, and they split the work like a restaurant: the part you see and tap, the part that thinks, and the part that remembers.

In plain words
Frontend, Backend, Database, API

Frontend = what you see and tap. Backend = the server that does the thinking. Database = where your history is stored. API = the agreed way the frontend asks the backend for things.

In plain words
Cloud hosting (Vercel & Railway)

Instead of a computer under a desk, the app lives on rented internet computers. Vercel hosts the part you see; Railway hosts the brain and the memory.

Computer vision — MediaPipe & OpenCV

While you practise, MediaPipe Pose (a free Google model) runs right inside your phone's browser and returns the 33 dots many times a second. AyuDrishti deliberately doesn't stop at a “this is Warrior II” label — those dots flow into a rule engine we wrote by hand, where NumPy turns them into real measurements: the angle at a knee, whether the spine is straight, the gap between a knee and an ankle, using eleven different measurement types.

Two customizations matter most. First, every distance is measured in torso-lengths, not pixels, so standing closer to the camera can't inflate your score. Second, if a body part isn't clearly visible, the rules that depend on it are switched off and the app lowers its confidence rather than inventing a number. On the desktop developer tools, OpenCV handles reading the webcam and drawing the skeleton on the video; the phone app uses MediaPipe's web build directly.

Two very different kinds of “NLP”

Natural-language understanding shows up twice — and, on purpose, they work in opposite ways:

1. Reading your preferences. On the profile screen you can type something like “I'm a beginner, about 20 minutes, 4 times a week, but my knee hurts.” A small parser reads that with keywords and patterns — no AI and no internet call — and pulls out your level, session length, frequency, focus areas, and any injury mention. Making it not an AI model is the point: it's predictable, instant, private, and it always shows you what it found and waits for your “OK” before changing anything.

2. Answering your questions. The chat assistant is the opposite — it does use an AI — but it's kept on a short leash by RAG (Part 7). The lookup step uses TF-IDF (from scikit-learn) to score which of the app's own notes best match your question. We customized it with a light stemmer so “breathe,” “breathing,” and “breath” all count as one word, added two-word phrase matching, and a fallback so a question always gets some relevant notes. Only then does the Groq-hosted AI write the wording — using just those notes, and citing them.

Machine learning, used honestly

It would be easy to slap “AI recommendations” on the box — but the part that chooses your session is deliberately not a mysterious model. It's a transparent scorer that adds up named reasons (+6 supports your goal, +5.6 targets your weak spot…) and shows every one, because a plan you can see the reasoning behind is a plan you'll actually follow.

scikit-learn powers the honest ML that is here: the TF-IDF lookup above, and an optional pose-classifier that can stand in for the rule-based recognizer when a labelled dataset is available. And the “adapt” step reads your past scores to spot recurring weak areas and feeds them back into that same scorer — which is exactly how the loop from Part 1 closes.

Part 11

Is my data safe?

Security was designed into the structure, not bolted on. In plain terms:

  • Your images never leave your phone — only the 33 dot-numbers do, and only results (scores, corrections) are stored.
  • Your data is walled off. You can only ever see your own sessions; asking for someone else's gets the exact same “not found” as data that doesn't exist — so it can't be poked at.
  • Passwords are scrambled (a one-way process called bcrypt) — even the team can't read them.
  • Everything travels encrypted over HTTPS, the padlock in your browser.
  • Only the real website is allowed to talk to the server (a rule called CORS), and repeated login guesses get blocked — so passwords can't be brute-forced.
In plain words
Login token (JWT)

After you log in, the server gives your app a signed wristband. Every later request shows the wristband, so the server knows it's you — and no one else's wristband opens your data.

Part 12

What's real, and what's still a prototype

An honest scorecard — because a project that only lists its strengths isn't trustworthy.

Working today

Camera assessment, spoken corrections, voice control, the adaptive plan, progress tracking, the RAG chatbot, and the whole app deployed live on the web.

Still a prototype

The wrist band is fully built in code but hasn't been tested on a real circuit board yet; live camera inside a phone app (vs. the phone browser) needs one more piece.

Important

AyuDrishti is a practice aid, not a medical device. Its alignment targets are an experienced coach's judgement expressed as numbers — genuinely useful, and not a substitute for a qualified teacher, especially if you have an injury.
