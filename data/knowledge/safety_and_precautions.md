# Practising safely

## General principles

- **Warm up before deeper stretches or backbends.** Cold connective tissue
  is more prone to strain; a few minutes of gentle movement (like Cat-Cow or
  a few rounds of Sun Salutation) prepares the body for more demanding
  shapes.
- **Work within a pain-free range.** A stretching sensation is normal;
  sharp, pinching, or joint-level pain is a signal to back off immediately,
  not to push through. "No pain, no gain" does not apply to joint safety.
- **Protect the neck in inversions and backbends.** Avoid turning the head
  while weight-bearing on it (e.g. in Shoulderstand-family poses), and
  support cervical alignment rather than cranking into a backbend from the
  neck.
- **Knees track over ankles, not past them, in lunges and standing poses.**
  A front knee that collapses inward or drifts far past the ankle loads the
  joint in a way it isn't built for under sustained hold.
- **Build up gradually.** Consistency over months matters more than
  intensity in any single session. A pose more than one level above current
  ability is where most avoidable strain happens.

## When to be more cautious, or to consult a professional first

- **Pregnancy.** Practice during pregnancy is generally considered safe for
  most people once cleared by a doctor or midwife, but deep twists, prone
  (belly-down) poses, intense core work, and poses that compress the
  abdomen are usually avoided, especially in the second and third
  trimester. A prenatal-specific class or a teacher informed about the
  pregnancy is strongly preferable to a generic routine.
- Recent surgery, or an existing joint or spine injury
- Uncontrolled high or low blood pressure (relevant to inversions and rapid
  breathing techniques like Kapalabhati)
- Glaucoma or detached retina (inversions increase pressure in the eyes)
- Any new or worsening pain during practice that doesn't resolve with rest

## What this app does and does not do

AyuDrishti measures posture against biomechanical reference ranges and
reports what it observes -- it does not diagnose conditions, and it is not
a substitute for guidance from a qualified instructor or a medical
professional, particularly around an existing injury. Its safety layer can
suggest a rest when posture strain and physiological signals (if a wearable
is connected) both indicate one, but the strongest action it ever takes is
that suggestion -- never a diagnosis.
